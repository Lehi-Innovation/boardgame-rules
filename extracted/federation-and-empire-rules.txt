INTRODUCTION
 INTRODUCTION
This product was created in the late summer of 2015 as a
means of explaining to gamers who have never seen the Fed-
eration & Empire game system what it is and how it works. Think
of F&E as World War II in Space.
This is not a “playable” product; you would need a rulebook,
map, and counters to actually play Federation & Empire. That
would be far too many pages and too much other material for a
product of this type to include.
This product was assembled mostly from items already pub-
lished in various Federation & Empire products. The intention is
to give a player a good overview of just how the game works,
what it includes, and how its rules combined to reflect a massive
strategic conflict between vast interstellar empires. The included
pages may reference items on pages not included in About F&E.
Federation & Empire is not that complicated of a game, but
it is big in that the map is five feet long and some of the more
advanced scenarios can include thousands of playing pieces. It
was, you can imagine, designed for people who like such games.
We do recognize, however, that lots of people prefer their games
in smaller bites, and the various products include smaller sce-
narios (usually just two empires for a few turns) that can be played
by two players in a long evening.

CONCEPTS USED IN THE GAME
GENERAL CONCEPTS AND SCALE
Every counter is one ship (or sometimes a small group of
ships). Every hex is 500 parsecs (about 1300 light years) across.
Every turn is six months, during which both sides attack and de-
fend the front line, collect tax revenue, build and repair ships,
and do a few other things.
EXPANSIONS
The boxed version of FEDERATION & EMPIRE is just a start-
ing point. While you can play it on its own for many years without
running out of new strategies and tactics to try, when you find
yourself wanting more things to do, the expansions are there for
you. The basic game is balanced, and whatever is added by each
expansion keeps the balance where it is. In some cases, the
basic list of availble units includes more and different ships.
While every expansion focuses on a specific theme, it in-
cludes many special rules (often unrelated to that theme).
Fighter Operations adds more carriers.
Combined Operations adds ground troops.
Advanced Operations adds X-ships.
Planetary Operations adds colonies.
Strategic Operations adds special new ships and rules.
ISC War adds the Inter-Stellar Concordium
Minor Empires adds three smaller empires (Vudar, LDR,
Seltorian) that are not portrayed in other products.
A GAME ABOUT MONEY
At its core, Federation and Empire is a game about money.
Every turn, you will get some “economic points” from your tax-
payers (and those on captured planets) and spend those points
to buy ships, repair ships, construct bases, convert ships of one
type into another, conduct special missions, and other things.
You will never have enough money and will have to prioritize
what you need to spend.
PROVINCES
While the map is in hexes, these are grouped together into
provinces (each five, six, or seven hexes). Provinces serve one
accounting function (each provinces produces a couple of eco-
nomics points per turn) and a game function (one enemy war-
ship in any hex of that province and the income drops by half). If
the enemy forces all of your ships and other units out of the prov-
ince then he gets half of the income (the other half disappears),
leading to a concept of “province raiding” wherein either player
might send one ship into a province just to deny the other player
income.
THE SEQUENCE OF PLAY
In a nutshell, you start the turn collecting tax money, then
spend most of it buying ships which are placed in the shipyard
hexes. Ships damaged earlier are repaired and made ready for
combat. You then move your ships, which creates battle hexes.
These are then resolved [which usually ends in one fleet (stack)
of ships retreating to an adjacent empty hex]. After combat, ships
can use “retrograde movement” to withdraw to bases.
MOVEMENT, PINNING, AND REACTION
Ships (usually stacks of them) move from one hex to an
adjacent one until they run out of movement (usually six hexes
per turn, but some units are faster or slower) or run into the en-
emy and decide to fight. Strategic movement (jumping from one
base to another one six hexes away and then another and an-
other) can be used to move ships from one side of your empire to
the other, but ships moving in this way cannot enter hexes con-
taining enemy ships.
When your ships enter a hex containing enemy ships, this
becomes a “battle hex” to be resolved in the Combat Phase.

Space is vast, and the most powerful starship can use its
weapons to control one thousanth of one percent of the hex it is
sitting in. Your ships can, literally, fly right through a hex contain-
ing a vast enemy fleet and attack something behind it. What stops
this is the concept of “pinning” the moving enemy ships.
“Pinning” means that for every enemy ship in a hex, you
have to leave one ship behind in that hex before the rest of your
fleet can slip through and attack something behind them. Smaller
fleets cannot slip through bigger fleets.
Normally, if it is your turn to move, you move your ships and
the enemy ships do not move, but there are two exceptions. One
is interception, in that if you move beside an enemy fleet, the
ships in that hex can “react” into the hex you are passing through
and “pin” some of your ships, creating a battle hex. The other
concept is reserve fleets. Each side had a very few of these, and
they can move during the Reserve Movement Step to add their
power to a battle hex that was just created. In this way, if you
don’t know which target the main enemy force will attack this
turn, you can hold a dozen ships behind the lines and move them
to the target that actually gets attacked.
COMBAT, COMMAND LIMITS, AND BATTLE ROUNDS
At the start of the Combat Phase, the attacker picks a battle
hex and resolves it. (As a losing battle here may block the retreat
path of a fleet over there you want to pick carefully which hex to
resolve first.) Each player then removes all of his ship from the
map and spreads them out on an empty part of the table, and
forms a battle force.
Obviously, bigger fleets have the advantage, but this is lim-
ited by the problem of command. That is to say, you may have
100 ships against 47 of his, but you cannot use all of your ships
at one time, only one battle force at a time. Every ship has a
command rating from 0 to 10; you pick a flagship and then in-
clude as many more ships as the command limit of the flagship.
A battle force led by a dreadnought could be as big as 11 ships,
while a battle force led by a mere heavy cruiser would be limited
to nine. (There are some ways to increase command limits, but
the maximum fleet tops out at about 15 using every trick in the
book.) While both fleets (regardless of their overall size) will field
more or less equal battle groups, a bigger fleet will be able to
replace losses and grind down the smaller enemy. Smaller fleets
often retreat after a round or two just to save their ships for an-
other turn.
You add up all of the attack factors in the battle force. You
and your opponent each pick a battle intensity number from one
to four and add them. Higher numbers mean more intense battles
and more losses. You then roll one die and cross-index it with the
battle intensity to produce a percentage. You then multiply that
percentage by your total attack factors and produce damage
points. You can use some of those (at double cost) to cripple or
kill a particular enemy ship; any more are then resolved by the
other player who must cripple or destroy his own ships to “pay
the piper” for that battle.
Warships are printed on double-sided counters, the back-
side representing a crippled ship about half strength. It is cheaper
and faster to repair a crippled ship than to build a new one, but
you only have so many repair facilities so you must carefully bal-
ance how many ships you cripple and how many you destroy.
Players then form another battle force and repeat the pro-
cess until someone gives up (or is wiped out). If surviving units
retreat, the victorious units can try to pursue them and destroy
any crippled ships before they reach repair facilities.
BASES AND GROUND UNITS
Anyone familiar with the source material has heard of star-

bases, huge fortresses in space where ships are commanded,
repaired, supplied, and even built. There are several kinds of
smaller bases, some of which can be moved and assembled
where you want them.
Your bases and planets combine to create the “supply grid”
which is used to move tax money to your capital and supplies
(fuel, food, spare parts, consumable munitions) to your fleets.
Ships more than six hexes from the supply grid are “out of sup-
ply” and fight at half strength. Bases are the stepping stones of
strategic movement, and are the destinations for fleets doing ret-
rograde movement. Bases are also capable of providing repairs.
There are many other “soft” units in the game, including fleet
repair docks (which repair ships but are fragile targets and move
very slowly), convoys (which extend the supply grid, but which
are vulnerable), tugs (which can move mobile bases, tow dam-
aged starships, and construct or upgrade bases).
Only the smallest bases can be built; anything larger is cre-
ated by upgrading a smaller base, which takes a tug (or some
other special unit, plus time and money. Obviously, bigger bases
are harder to kill. It can take an enemy fleet many battle rounds
to score damage steps on a starbase and kill it.
Certain units can be built on planets. Think “shore batteries”
of cannons firing at enemy ships attacking the planet. Capturing
an enemy planet requires killing all of the ground units on it.
Ground troops (in one of the expansions) can help here.
CAPITAL SYSTEMS
The hex containing your capital planet includes many other
planets (sometimes 10 of them) and the main shipyard. Rules
provide for attacks on capital systems, which break the fight down
into several smaller battles (one for each star system). Since
bases and ground defense are added to the battle force outside
of the command limits, this makes wearing down enemy defenses
a very expensive proposition. Expect to lose a lot of ships.
