Boarding Party — David Lent solo/competitive gameplay reference

BOARDING PARTY — THIRD-PARTY GAMEPLAY RULES REFERENCE; ENGLISH
David Lent, The Centurion’s Review, December25,2017 page metadata.
Solo and competitive two-player procedures remain distinct. Not the full original manual/tables.
Exact initial robot deployment, all movement/weapon tables and complete two-player scoring remain missing.

Boarding Party is an old microgame from Task Force Games that was released in 1982.  It can be played solitaire or with two players.  The basic premise is that the spaceship Ulysses and its human crew enter combat with a Destructors spaceship – a robot ship programmed to eliminate all forms of life.  Both ships are disabled early in the battle and it is up to the crew of the Ulysses to board the enemy spacecraft and fight past the robot crew to get to its command computer and destroy it before the robots can repair the ship.

The components in Boarding Party are a 12-page rulebook, map and 54 counters (some of which are blank).

The map has all 4 levels of the robot spaceship and the primary features are locked doors, shock doors, upward gravity shafts and downward gravity shafts.  There is also a combat results table printed on it along with a defensive chart, which has “yes” or “no” counters to show if doors are locked on a certain level or if the shock doors are active on a specific level.

Victory conditions in this game depend on whether you are playing the solitaire or two-player scenario.  In the solitaire game, you have to either destroy the command computer by turn 14 or destroy all the repair robots.  The two-player game is won by the player with the most victory points.  Victory points for the two-player game are awarded for controlling the command computer at the end of the game and for destroying enemy units.

The gameplay in the solitaire scenario is very simple.  The humans choose which airlock they will enter the ship at and then randomly place “yes” and “no” markers on the automated defense chart.  Next, he rolls to see how many blast packs he will get.  The robots are setup in certain places according to the rulebook at the beginning of the game.  During the game, the humans move through the ship and the robots move randomly.   If a human goes through a shock door and it turns out to be activated, he is killed unless he is an electronics specialist.  The electronics specialist can wait by the door to keep it from triggering while the rest of the crew goes through it.  If a door turns out to be locked, an explosives expert must use one of his blast packs to blow it up.  If the humans enter any room with robots a fight occurs.

Combat in this game is extremely simple and simultaneous.  You first consult the rulebook to see what weapons the humans and robots are armed with (obviously, it would have been too difficult to print this on the counters).  Next, you roll for each weapon and look it up on the combat results table to see the result.  Humans with blasters are the first to get killed in groups of more than one human.  Robots generally shoot to kill, but slaver robots will try to incapacitate a human with a whip if there is only one human present.  If slaver robots encounter any unconscious humans, they will capture them.

When the humans reach the command computer an alarm sounds and all the robots start moving towards that room.  The humans first have to have at least one human shooting at each robot in the room, but the rest of the humans may fire on the computer.  It takes a total of 30 damage to destroy the computer.

The two-player game is pretty much the same as the solitaire game, except each turn the robot player may directly control 3 robots.  The rest have to move randomly though.  In this scenario, you do not destroy the computer and instead try to control the computer room at the end of the game.
