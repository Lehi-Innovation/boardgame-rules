Introduction
Overview of Play
Players start as one of four (or five, with the Expansion) nondescript archetypes, but can mutate or branch out new species from this basic type by bidding on DNA or genotype cards as they are revealed. Each player's population grows and is counted when the Mesozoic cards are all played, and again when the Cenozoic cards are gone. The player with the highest total wins.
Game Scale
Each epoch card represents 6 million years. The game is one galactic year long (260 million years, the amount of time it takes the solar system to orbit once about the center of the Milky Way). Each hex represents a physiographic region 1500 km across. Each biomass point represents 2000 megatons of vegetation, arthropods, or sea-food. Each genotype tent represents either 30 megatons of herbivores, or 1 megaton of predatory animals. A "megaton" is a million metric tons, where each metric ton is 1000 kg.
American Megafauna Version 2.5.
This rulebook combines the Basic and Advanced game rules of the 2nd edition and its expansion into one streamlined set of rules. Rules that were in various parts of the old rulebook (such as the Glossary-Index) have been collected and rationalized, while non-rule information has been moved to the endnotes. The rules are designed to work with all existing components. All "Advanced Rule" references on the epoch cards are in effect when using this rulebook.
Parts
P1. List
The Second Edition of American Megafauna allows up to four people to play, and includes these components:
1 Rulebook
1 Map
188 Epoch Cards
120 DNA Tents, 224 Population Tents
1 Timeline / Game Tips Sheet
4 Physiology Sheets
The American Megafauna Expansion (referred to in these rules as the "Expansion") adds the Proto-Croc (a.k.a. Arizonasaurus) and increases the maximum number of players to five. The following components are included:
46 Epoch Cards
27 DNA Tents, 56 Population Tents
1 Physiology Sheet
Dice are not needed to play this game. To represent the gene pool, about 25 markers (such as pennies or washers, not included) per player are required. To prepare for play, print out the physiology sheets at the end of this rulebook, cut apart the cards, and cut apart and fold the playing pieces ("tents").
P2. Cards
Starburst cards (those marked with rays as shown in Setting Up The Game) depict survivors of the Permian Extinctions. Starburst cards are set aside after setup but before play begins. The remaining cards (called epoch cards) determine the course of the game. The five types of epoch cards are Biome, DNA, Genotype, Immigrant, or Catastrophe.
a. Expansion Cards.
The four "Technology" cards added in the Expansion (Clothing, Hand-Axe, Hafted Weaponry, and Fire-Bearing) are identified by an eight-pointed-star background. They do not use any adaptation slots when expressed. Place them at the bottom of the expressing genotype's slots. Technology cards cannot be inherited, and require their genotype to have Tool Use expressed.
Some of the cards added in the Expansion are described below:
Hand-Axe [#213]:
This card wins all predator dentition culls, due to bone marrow scavenging. Think of such creatures as having "zero" dentition, for predator tie-breakers.
Poisonous Fangs DNA [#196] and Hafted Weaponry [#214]:
These are especially helpful for predators. Spear-armed and fang-armed creatures ignore the armor, swiftness, and nocturnal defenses of their prey.
Fire-bearing [#215]:
Allows creatures to ignore the "extinction calculus" found on some catastrophe cards, and increases their migration range to three.
Symbiotic Bacteria DNA [#222]:
Allows a genotype to win herbivore-dentition culls against all other genotypes. In effect, the genotype has seven teeth, defeating even immigrants.

The number of letters in the dentition formula is a vital survival factor. For instance, mmrrr with five letters has more than the four of rrrr.

a. Genotype Columns.
The two left-most columns of the Physiology Sheet are associated with the player's starting genotype, called its archetype. The remaining three columns are used for up to three more genotypes the player may obtain by winning auctions during the game.
b. Size Dial.
The rotation of the genotype card in this track records the size of adult individuals in the population (from 40 kg to 60 tons). The sizes are abstracted from one to six, as indicated by the red pointer in the corner of the genotype card.
P4. DNA Tents
Each DNA tent records a genotype's inherited attributes. These tents correspond to DNA cards and are placed on the genotype cards during the game to indicate the ways in which they have evolved. The following list shows the DNA codes and their meanings:
A = Armor piercing
a = armored*
B = Browse (ability to eat trees)
G = Graze (ability to digest grass and shrubs)
H = Husker (ability to shell mast)
I = Insectivorid (arthropod-eating ability)
M = Marine (swimming adaptations)
N = Nocturnal* (burrowing or nighttime senses)
P = Physiology (behavior and climate adaptations)
S = Swiftness*
W = Wild (player choice; see Stage B1e and B1f)
sex = sexual intimidation or advertising
wings = may migrate three and fly over seas, ice-sheets, and bridges.
* Roadrunner DNA: "Roadrunner" is a term used to refer to any herbivore inedible due to armor, speed, and/or nocturnal DNA.
P5. Map
The map is divided into 11 hexagons (hexes). Each hex is divided into two slots, plus three triangular areas, one over and two under the slots. Between the hexes are displacement arrows and borderlands.
Each row of hexes is called a latitude. The map has five latitudes (hex rows), labeled "arctic circle", "jet stream", "horse latitudes", and two "tropical". For instance, the two northernmost hexes ("Beringia" and "Greenland") are in the Arctic Circle latitude. The southernmost latitudes, "tropical", have three hexes (Sierra Madre, Gulf of Mexico, and Panama).
Each latitude is marked on the western map edge with a climate preference icon, either "dry", "wet", or "seasonal"; see preferred latitude (Stage A1).
a. Borderlands.
The ten outlying areas are treated as hexes with one slot and both a predator and husker triangle. Any number of biomes may stack in a borderland (retaining their biomass), but only the uppermost is active. Genotypes or biomes may migrate in or out of borderlands only along displacement arrows. The Asian and South American borderlands can store immigrants waiting to emigrate into America.
b. Bridge.
A land isthmus between two continents used for terrestrial migrations. The three bridges in the game are Cocos, Kula, and Cantabria. A marker placed on a bridge location indicates flooding, causing displacement and migration limitations. Bridges can flood or clear due to Greenhouse or Milankovich changes, or a special catastrophe card. If the Cocos or Kula bridge clears, each immigrant (starting with the top) stacked in South America or Asia will invade per Stage A4c-A4f.
c. Displacement Arrow.
Each slot and borderland has one arrow exiting to the north, and another to the south. These indicate where to displace biomes during Greenhouse climate changes.
d. Husker Triangle.
A triangular region below each slot, two per hex. Animals that are eating mast are placed in this area. Only a biome with the acorn icon beneath its biomass numbers has mast. A husker triangle can support as many husker population tents as the biomass of the biome. The triangle's DNA requirements are "H", and the niche is whatever the niche of the biome above it is.
e. Mountain-Building Slots.
Slots marked with an orange border are mountain-building slots. Orogeny biomes are restricted to these slots. Orogeny biome cards must be played in mountain-building slots, and never displace nor grow. Marine biomes are blocked from being placed or migrated into these slots. Exception: A marine biome can exist in a mountain-building slot if it is designated to start there at the beginning of the game. If the biome card is displaced out of its initial location, all normal rules apply (i.e., it can no longer be placed in a mountain-building slot).
f. Predator Triangle.
The triangular region in each hex above the slots. All tents placed here become predatory. "Predator" is a synonym for carnivorous meat-eating, including scavenging, in this game.
g. Slots.
Either of the two card-sized squares in a hex. Biome cards are stored in either the east or west slot. An empty slot is terrestrial (land) if the Greenhouse Level is below 300 ppm, otherwise it is marine. A hex with both slots flooded may block migration; see migration limitations (Stage F1).
P6. Genotype Tents
Each player has four sets of 14 population tents which are distinguished from those of other players by dentition. The four different animal pictures on the tents match the four genotype columns on the player's Physiology Sheet.
P7. Timeline
This sheet is used to store the epoch cards before they are revealed. Each card played from the timeline represents an epoch (one game turn). After 29 turns (30 turns in a 4- or 5-player game), as shown on the timeline, the era changes from the Mesozoic to the Cenozoic.
Setting Up the Game
a. Choose Game Length.
The standard game, covering the period from the Triassic to the Quaternary, lasts 41 turns. For a shorter game, players may start in later eras: Jurassic (35 turns), Cretaceous (25 turns), or Tertiary (12 turns).
Note: The later eras are more challenging because there are fewer starting biomes without DNA starting requirements: The Triassic has 11 biomes with no requirements, the Jurassic 9, the Cretaceous 8, and the Tertiary 7.
b. Place Starting Biomes and Map Markers.
Lay out the map in the middle of the playing area so that it is accessible to all players. Reading down the correct column on the Setup Table (located at the end of this rulebook), place biomes and markers as follows:
Biomes:
Each biome card is placed in the slot shown on the Setup Table. The number in parentheses after the card name is the card number. Where multiple cards are listed in one slot, cards are stacked in the order listed (i.e., place the first card listed at the top of the stack). All biome cards are initially placed so the "2" biomass value is pointing northward (i.e., the text on the card reads right-side up).
Bridges:
If the land bridges are "Flooded," place markers on all three bridges. Otherwise, leave the bridges empty.
Milankovich Cycle and Greenhouse Level:
Place markers on the listed Milankovich Cycle and Greenhouse Level positions.
Example: If starting in the Triassic, place card 177 in Beringia East, card 175 in Greenland West, and so on for all slots listed in the Triassic column. All bridges are clear, so no markers are placed on the map. One marker is placed in each of three top Milankovich Cycle positions, and lastly, a marker is placed in the 300 ppm Greenhouse Level position.
c. Choose Physiology Sheet and Archetype.
Each player receives one Physiology Sheet at random (or each may choose his favorite if all players agree) and the associated archetype card. Place the archetype card in the leftmost size dial of the Physiology Sheet so that its upper-left corner arrow points to size number 1.
Note: It is recommended that rm not play against mmrrr in a two player game. This is because the mmrrr two-tusker player would win both mammal and reptile tie-breakers.
d. Receive Starting Gene Markers.
Each player starts the game with five genes (markers). Extra markers are put into a communal gene pool.
Note: If using the Expansion's Proto-Croc, the Proto-Croc player starts with ten genes instead of five, if the Dino-Croc (dentition rm) is also in the game. This is because the Proto-Croc is disadvantaged when pitted against the Dino-Croc.
e. Shuffle and Deal Cards.
Set aside any unused "starburst" cards. The remaining cards are the epoch cards. Mix them well and then randomly deal cards to form face-down stacks as indicated on the Timeline. Do not deal cards to any unused stacks (i.e., if the game starts in the Jurassic, do not deal any cards to the Triassic stack). The remaining cards will not be used in this game.
Note: For the 4- and 5-player game, deal out one additional card to one of the stacks in the Mesozoic.
f. Place Starting Populations.
Each player locates the 56 genotype population tents with his dentition on them. Fourteen of these are taller than the rest; these are his archetype population tents. In player order each player places one archetype tent on a biome card on the map. This must be one reading "NONE" in the corners of the biome (see requirements).
S1. Player Order
Any player may request that any setup phase or epoch stage be performed in order from the most r dentition to the least r dentition. Thus, the order is: rrrr, mmrrr, rr 1/2m (available in the Expansion), rm, and finally mmm.
S2. Late Arrivals / Early Departures
a. Late Arrivals:
If a player arrives after the game has started, he gets an unused Physiology Sheet and its archetype card, and is paid his five genes. He may immediately use these genes to purchase, for one gene each, any DNA or genotype cards discarded during the game. The player then places one population tent in any suitable biome or triangle.
b. Early Departures:
If a player leaves the game, his population tents remain in place. They compete, adjust size and population, and follow biome displacements as immigrants; see Stages A4f and A4g.
Playing the Game
Each turn represents one epoch and is divided into nine Stages, which are played in the order listed below. If necessary, Stage activities may be performed in player order.
Stage A: Reveal Epoch Card
– Turn over and resolve the topmost epoch card of the earliest deck on the Timeline. Note: If playing with 4 or 5 players, draw two cards instead of one, resolving the first before drawing the second. Ignore "Genetic Drift" if it appears on the second card.
Stage B. Express Cards
– DNA and genotype cards held by a player may be "expressed" (placed face-up and in play on his Physiology Sheet).
Stage C. Recess DNA
– Cards in play on his Physiology Sheet may be "recessed" (removed from his Physiology Sheet and held in his hand).
Stage D. Adjust Trophic Level and Size
– Each player may change the diet and size of his genotypes.
Stage E. Population Expansion
– Each player may add one population tent in every biome or triangle where each genotype already has one or more tents.
Stage F. Herbivore Migrations
– Each herbivore may move up to its maximum range.
Stage G. Carnivore Migrations and Final Size
– Each predator may move up to its maximum range, and then change its size.
Stage H. Herbivore Cull
– Reduce herbivore population, if necessary.
Stage I. Carnivore Cull
– Reduce carnivore population, if necessary.
Note: If all players agree, Stages B through I can be skipped. This can occur if the new card does not change the ecology and all populations are at maximum; in that case there may be no reason to go through each sequence.
End of Turn Checks
Players should check their opponent's game pieces occasionally at the end of turn for the following:
a.
Remove extra herbivores tents if there are more than the biomass of the biome.
b.
Remove any predator unable to consume its prey due to roadrunner DNA or size.
c.
Reduce the biomass of the lesser climax biome if the hex capacity is exceeded.
d.
Recess any DNA cards outside their size range per Stage D2b.
Stage A. Reveal Epoch Card
Turn over the topmost epoch card of the earliest deck on the Timeline. It will be a Biome, DNA, Genotype, Immigrant, or Catastrophe.
Note: If playing with 4 or 5 players, draw two cards instead of one, resolving the first before drawing the second. (This gives on average one adaptation per player in the first four turns.) Important: Ignore "Genetic Drift" if it appears on the second card.
A1. Biome Card
A biome card genetically enriches each player plus introduces a new environment onto the map. The four kinds of biomes are terrestrial (tan), marine (blue), orogeny (dark brown), and ice-sheets (white).
Biomass.
The biomass of a biome is the number on the card edge oriented to read upright. Each biome can support a maximum number of herbivore tents equal to its biomass.
Climate Preference.
The climate specialization of a biome card, which raises or lowers its biomass during Milankovich changes. A biome is in its preferred latitude if its preference matches the icon of its latitude; see the map west edge.
Climax.
All biomes are rated on a climax scale from 1 to 99, i.e., from the least to the most stable.
Ice-Sheets.
An ice biome card is restricted to biomass of four, and thus dominates the entire hex. It causes migration and displacement limitations.
Marine.
A type of sea-life biome identified by a blue card. Marine biomes cannot be placed or displaced into a mountain-building slot. Note: Certain marine biomes start the game in orogeny slots. This is an allowable exception to the normal rules; see P5e.
Mast.
Biomes that have the acorn icon behind each biomass number contain mast, available to those with H DNA. Mast biomass does not count towards the general hex capacity of four.
Niche.
The DNA or size, as listed in all four corners of a biome card (in the square after the requirements), that defines the fittest animal in a competition for that biome.
Preferred Latitude.
The latitude having a climate (see the western map edge) matching the climate preference icon in the lower right corner of the biome card.
Requirements.
The DNA, as listed in the corners of a biome card, required for a herbivore to exploit that card. A herbivore needs "MM" DNA to move to the biome shown below. Cards marked "NONE" have no requirements.
a. Genetic Enrichment.
When a biome card is turned up, each player receives one gene. Exception: Players who have sex DNA on at least one genotype receive two genes. There is no advantage to having more than one sex card or more than one genotype with sex.
b. Placement Latitude.
Place the biome card in a slot on the map. It may only be placed in a slot (not borderland) in the latitude indicated on its card. Biomes marked "any" are cosmopolitan and can go into any latitude.
c. Relief.
Orogeny biomes (dark brown) can only be put in mountain-building slots. Marine biomes (blue) cannot be placed in mountain-building slots.
d. Lowest Climax.
If several destination slots exist, the new biome replaces the existing biome having the lowest climax. Empty slots count as zero climax. The replaced card becomes a displaced biome (see below).
Note: Biomes which are at biomass 4 fill both slots, thus there is no empty slot in that hex.
e. Stillborn.
If the climax of the new biome is lower than that of the biomes in all legal slots, then the new biome is extinct and is removed from the game.
f. Ties.
If there is more than one equally qualifying slot, then the slot which is furthest south, and if still tied, furthest west is favored.
g. Hex Capacity.
Except for orogeny cards, the new biome is rotated to fill the remainder of the space in the hex, i.e., so that the uppermost numbers of both biomes add up to four.
Displaced Biomes.
Move a displaced biome into an empty slot in the same hex. If none are available, then move it to an empty slot in an adjacent hex or borderland.
a. Extinction.
A displaced biome with no legal slot to go to is removed from the game.
b. Ties.
If there is more than one equally qualifying slot, then the slot which is furthest south, and if still tied, furthest west is favored.
c. Biomass.
After moving the biome to the empty slot, rotate it so its biomass fills the remaining hex capacity up to four.
d. Displacement Limitations.
Biomes are blocked from displacement by the following instances: if a marine biome would displace into a mountain-building slot; orogeny biomes never displace (biomes displacing into their slot must compete with them); if displacing would exit the map, cross a flooded bridge, or enter a hex containing an ice-sheet.
A2. DNA Card
DNA is coded A, a, B, G, H, I, M, N, P, S, W, X, "sex" or "wings" in this game; see the DNA Table on the map's east edge. The dominant DNA in an allele is indicated by the use of a DNA tent. A DNA card represents new animal characteristics that a player can add to one of his existing genotypes.
a. Auction.
Players interested in acquiring a DNA card must bid for it in an open auction by placing a number of gene markers they are willing to spend on the card. They may increase, but not decrease, their bid during the auction. The minimum bid is one gene. Players are not allowed to sell, donate, or exchange cards or genes. The player with the highest bid pays his genes to the pool and takes the card into his hand. Losing bidders spend nothing. If no one bids on a DNA card, it is discarded.
b. Resolving Bidding Ties.
The player who has more of the teeth specified on the card tie-break section wins the bid.
Example: The "Trunk" card is labeled "auction tie-break: m", so that in a bidding war the mmm player would win over the rrrr player and the rm player if all three bid only one gene. This reflects a mammalian superiority in trunk development.
A3. Genotype Card
A genotype card represents a new animal type that is being spawned off from one of the player's existing types, inheriting some of the parent's characteristics.
Amphibian.
Animals with exactly one M DNA and thus able to enter both terrestrial and marine biomes. Genotypes with no M DNA cannot go into all sea hexes or the Atlantic, and genotypes with more than one M DNA cannot leave marine biomes.
Heritage DNA.
Each genotype card lists innate DNA called heritage DNA. Heritage DNA as encoded on the genotype cards is distinct from that appended by DNA cards and tents. For instance, primates have "PPW" as heritage, reflecting their big brains, but may augment this with wings, humps, spines, etc.
Dynasty.
Each genotype card is split into two halves (flying reptiles and bats in the example shown); see Stage B2a.
a. Auction.
Players interested in acquiring a genotype card must bid for it in an open auction by placing a number of gene markers they are willing to spend on the card. They may increase, but not decrease, their bid during the auction. The minimum bid is one gene. Players are not allowed to sell, donate, or exchange cards or genes. The player with the highest bid pays his genes to the pool and takes the card into his hand. Losing bidders spend nothing. If no one bids on a Genotype card, it is discarded.
b. Resolving Bidding Ties.
If the Greenhouse Level is 300 ppm or higher, the player with the most r dentition always wins auction ties for genotype cards. If the Greenhouse Level is 200 ppm or lower, the player with the most m dentition wins ties instead.
Example: The Beak Lizard and the Two-tusker players each put a gene on a revealed genotype card. The greenhouse is 200 ppm, so that if the auction stops now, the Two-tusker will win. Beak lizard puts a second gene on it. Then a new player, Dog-face, decides to put down two genes. Nobody goes higher, so Dog-face with mmm dentition wins the card for two genes.
A4. Immigrant Card
The appearance of an immigrant card causes genetic drift, plus an independent predator or herbivore invades a biome or triangle.
a. Genetic Drift.
All players with card(s) in their hand select one to discard.
b. Era.
Use the immigrant that corresponds to the era in which it was drawn (see Timeline).
c. Entry.
Immigrants start their migration from the Atlantic Rift, South America, or Asia borderland, as marked on their card.
d. Passage.
Immigrants check hexes and borderlands from west to east, until a suitable one is found. Immigrants from Asia check the northern latitudes first, and move south, while immigrants from South America or the Atlantic check southern latitudes first. Discard the card if no suitable slots exist.
Immigrants are subject to all migration limitations except that their range is infinite. If blocked from entry by a flooded bridge, then store the card in the borderland; it will not invade (and is not in play) until the bridge clears.
Immigrants blocked from entering the map because of a flooded bridge are stacked so that the most recent is on the top. When the bridge clears, find biomes for them from top to bottom. (This will tend to place the oldest immigrants farthest from the bridge, simulating the progressive radiation out from the bridge.)
e. Herbivore.
Immigrants with six teeth (rrrrrr or mmmmmm) are herbivores. They will stop at the first slot with biome suitability, using their "W" if required to match the biome requirements. If they do not need the "W" for requirements, they will change it to the niche DNA. (Put a DNA tent on the card to indicate this choice.)
Note: Herbivore immigrants will not stop at a biome containing other immigrant herbivores. Only immigrants with H heritage may attempt to become huskers. The immigrant "W" DNA cannot be converted into "H" DNA.
Example: Rays [#157] appear. With more than one M DNA, they are confined to marine biomes. The southernmost marine biome is primitive fish [#178], with both a requirement and niche of M. The ray card goes on the fish, with an M DNA tent added.
f. Carnivore.
Immigrants that have a single letter dentition (either r or m) are predators. They check each predator triangle, seeking herbivore slots only if no triangles on the map have prey suitability. They will change their "W" to a roadrunner DNA if required to catch a prey item. (Put a DNA tent on the card to indicate this choice.) If the "W" is not needed, it is not used and cannot be changed later. They continually adopt the largest size (in their range) that is suitable for their prey.
Note: Carnivore immigrants may prey on herbivore immigrants. They will not stop at hexes with other immigrant predators, however.
g. Migrant Population.
The immigrant population continually becomes the largest that can be supported in their hex. The presence of this number of immigrants is shown by either having their card in that biome, or by using spare population tents.
Immigrant cards or tents remain (continually following prey migrations or biome displacements) until they are culled by competition, or are wiped out by a catastrophe.
Since immigrant herbivores constantly follow their biomes, if their biome displaces into a borderland, immigrants following it automatically arrive first and select this biome. If more than one immigrant herbivore follows their biomes into a borderland, a winner is randomly selected, and the others go extinct.
A5. Catastrophe Card
The appearance of a catastrophe card causes a calamity that genetically enriches the survivors.
a. Genetic Enrichment.
Each player receives five genes. Exception: Players who have sex DNA on at least one genotype receive additional genes. In a two player game, they receive six genes. In games with three or more players, they receive 10 genes. There is no advantage to having more than one sex card or more than one genotype with sex.
b. Era.
Use the catastrophe that corresponds to the era in which it was drawn (see Timeline).
c. Type.
Each catastrophe is a greenhouse, pandemic, bridge, or extraterrestrial event.
Greenhouse.
A marker in the chart on the west map edge monitors the Earth's Greenhouse Level.
a. Plant Growth.
If the level reaches 600 ppm, the highest climax non-orogeny biome in each hex increases biomass until the hex capacity of four is reached. Note: A biome swelling due to a 600 ppm greenhouse only expands to fill unused hex capacity. It does not drive its neighboring biome extinct.
b. Bridge.
A change in the level may trigger certain bridges to clear or flood as indicated.
c. End of Game.
If the greenhouse goes over 600 ppm or below 150 ppm, the game ends as a result of greenhouse levels. Play the rest of the current game turn (i.e., through Stage I) before ending the game.
d. Biome Migration.
If the greenhouse goes up, all biomes starting with the ice-sheets displace up one latitude sliding to the slot or borderland indicated by the northbound displacement arrow. If the greenhouse goes down, slide each biome to the slot or borderland indicated by its southern arrow. Displacement may be blocked by displacement limitations (see Stage A1). Immigrant herbivores displace with their biomes, while all other animals remain until migration (Stages F and G).
e. Biome Competition.
If more than one biome displaces into a slot, the highest climax card lives and the rest are discarded. If more than one biome displaces into a borderland, they are stacked in random order until the first herbivore enters the borderland per Stage F3.
f. Hex Capacity.
Check the amount of biomass in each hex. If any hex has more than four, rotate the lower climax biome downward until the total biomass reaches four.
g. Order of Effect.
Should a change in greenhouse result in multiple effects, apply them in this order: Bridges and hexes flood or clear, biomes displace north or south, and biomes swell to fill the hex capacity.
Pandemic.
Organisms require genes to summon the anti-bodies, etc. needed to fight disease and parasites.
a.
Among those having more than one genotype, the player (or players, if tied) with the fewest gene markers chooses one of his genotypes to lose all population and suffer extinction.
b.
Pandemic deaths are chosen in player order (see S1).
Bridge.
This event causes a bridge to clear or flood.
Extraterrestrial.
An extraterrestrial catastrophe refers to an event originating outside the earth that kills off overspecialized animals. An "extinction calculus" is printed on each extraterrestrial catastrophe card. Check each animal's DNA, including heritage. If the number of specified letters, in any combination, is greater than specified in the formula, it loses all population; see extinction (Stage H4).
Examples:
A comet impact has the extinction calculus ">3 AaBGHMS". An immigrant with BBBa would be killed, because it has more than three of the fatal DNA. A genotype with AAPPS is spared.
A solar flare has the extinction calculus "size > 4". A size five sea cow is doomed; a size four tyrannosaur is safe. It also has the calculus ">2 Aa". This will kill off triple-armored turtles.
Stage B. Express Cards
B1. Express DNA
Each player may add to the characteristics of their chosen genotypes by expressing DNA cards held in their hand.
a.
The genotype chosen must have an empty box in its column to place the DNA card into. Note that the empty slot must be of the correct location, either head (circle), guts (triangle), or limbs (diamond).
b.
The genotype chosen must be a size that is in the range specified on the DNA card.
Example: If the stegosaurs are at size five, they are not allowed to get feathers, which have a 1-4 size range.
c.
The heritage DNA of the genotype chosen must be compatible with any special notes on the DNA card.
Example: The "Saber Tooth" DNA card carries the legend "recesses all G cards. Not allowed with G heritage", so it cannot be expressed on genotypes (such as duckbills or camels) that have "G" heritage.
d.
Once expressed, place DNA tents of the appropriate letter(s) onto the genotype card in the size dial.
Example: If a player decides to give feathers (which are "PP") to his stegosaurs, then two "P" tents are put on the stegosaur genotype card.
e. Wild Card ("W") DNA.
Upon expressing a "W" DNA card, the player puts the single-letter DNA tent of his choice upon his genotype card. Once selected, this DNA is fixed, until it is recessed.
Tip: Later in the game, it may be useful to recess and express a "W" DNA card to change its expression.
f. Special Wild Card ("WW"):
DNA card #62 ("WW") may be changed during Stage D of every turn to any two chosen DNA letters. It cannot be expressed unless the "Tool-use" DNA card [#66] has been expressed first.
B2. Express Genotypes
Each player may diversify his lineages by expressing genotype cards. For each one played, the player chooses an existing genotype tent to be the parent for the new genotype.
Tip: If a player's archetype is extinct, his next genotype purchase can be expressed in his first column as a new genotype. This gives him room for six DNA.
a. Dynasty.
Each genotype card has a reptile half and a mammal half (see Stage A3). The dino-croc, beak lizard, and (if using the Expansion) the proto-croc players must use the reptile side, and the two-tusker and dog-face must use the mammal side.
b. Size.
Orient the new genotype card on the Physiology Sheet to the size of its parent genotype. This must be within the size range specified on the genotype card. (In other words, if the genotype is outside this size range, then the card cannot be played.)
c. Inheritance.
The new genotype will inherit all non-heritage DNA from the existing genotype, excepting incompatibilities and size limitations as listed on the DNA card. DNA inherited from one's ancestor does not count towards the one DNA card limit on each head, guts, or limbs location. Some DNA cards have special abilities (such as wings, sex, care of young, etc.). These abilities are inherited along with the DNA letter.
Examples:
A genotype diverging from a saber-toothed trunked archetype receives the DNA from both these head cards and still has an empty head slot that can hold an additional future head DNA card of its own.
A new hamster genotype cannot inherit the heritage "NPS" DNA of its feline parent. If the cat had sex, wings, and I DNA cards, these tents are inherited and put on the hamster card. If the feline had an S DNA card with a size range of 2-6, this tent is not put on a size one hamster.
d. Tent Choice.
Choose a population tent set not in use to represent the new population. Each player has a maximum of four genotypes that can be expressed.
e. Map Placement.
Replace one of the parent genotype tents on the map with a tent of the new genotype. Note: if this is the last tent for this genotype on the map, the parent goes extinct.
f. Milankovich Cycles.
As soon as a genotype card is expressed, a Milankovich Cycle, as listed in the center of the genotype card, is triggered. A "Milankovich P", for example, will shift the marker in the P track (see southeastern corner of the map) from the upper slot to the lower slot, or (if the marker is already in the lower slot), back to the upper. Each cycle executes (in the order shown on the chart) two Milankovich Events (see below).
Example: The primates card is expressed, which specifies "Milankovich T". If the marker in the T track is in the lower slot, it is slid to the upper, and the events referenced ("Erosion" and "Bridges") occur.
Milankovich Events
a. Land Drier.
This event shrinks each terrestrial biome having the climate preference of "wet". Rotate each card with the raindrops icon clockwise by 90 degrees to indicate a biomass one less. Biomes reduced below a biomass of one go extinct.
b. Land Wetter.
This event shrinks each terrestrial biome having the climate preference of "dry". Rotate the card clockwise 90 degrees.
c. Blooms.
This event grows each biome in its preferred latitude by one in biomass. If this exceeds the hex capacity, the biome in the neighboring slot reduces one. If both biomes in a hex are in their preferred latitude and the hex capacity is exceeded, the lower climax is reduced until the biomass is four.
d. Seasonal.
This event will cause any biome cards (land and sea) with the climate preference of "seasonal" to increase its biomass by one. If this exceeds the hex capacity, the biome in the neighboring slot reduces one. If both biomes in a hex have a seasonal preference and the hex capacity is exceeded, lower climax is reduced until the biomass is four.
e. Doldrums.
This event will cause all terrestrial biomes with the climate preference of "seasonal" to shrink by one biomass. This is due to a decreased difference between summer and winter.
f. Bridges.
Each bridge event changes the state of all three bridges. If a marker exists on a bridge, remove it. If it has no marker, one is put on (to show it is flooded). The state of each bridge persists until the next bridge event. A cleared bridge may unleash immigrants; see bridge (P5b).
g. Erosion.
This event reduces each orogeny biome by one biomass (due to the action of rain on the mountains).
h. Hypoxia.
This event reduces each marine biome by one biomass (due to lack of oxygen).
Note: A flooded bridge does not stop continental drift.
Stage C. Recess DNA
Through inheritance, a single DNA card may be shared by multiple genotypes. If a genotype has its shared DNA recessed, it loses the DNA tent, and the DNA card is moved to any other legal genotype that is still using it. All genotypes using that DNA retain their tents.
Each player holds their recessed cards face-up on the table in front of them. These cards are referred to as the player's "hand." There is no limit to the number of cards a player may hold in his hand.
C1. Voluntary Recession
A player may withdraw any number of DNA Cards from his Physiology Sheet to his hand. This removes the associated DNA tents from all genotypes using the recessed card.
Note: If a recessed DNA card is later re-expressed, it may be applied to the genotype of the player's choice per Stage B1.
C2. Forced Recession
Some DNA cards contain a statement that if they are expressed, they recess other DNA cards that genotype may have. (Other genotypes of a player are unaffected.) Recessed cards are returned to one's hand, and the associated DNA counters (on the genotype card) are removed.
Stage D. Adjust Trophic Level and Size
D1. Trophic Level
Each player may change the diet of his genotypes.
a. Plant-eaters become Meat-eaters.
To change a herbivore into a predator, shift its tents from the biome card or the husker triangle to the predator triangle in the same hex or borderland. (If there is no suitable prey in the hex, the carnivore must migrate or die this turn.)
b. Meat-eaters become Plant-eaters.
Predators that switch their trophic level to herbivory shift their population from the predator triangle to either of the two slots in the hex or either of the two husker triangles. (Again, if there are no suitable biomes in the hex, the tent must migrate or die this turn.)
Note: Herbivores suffer no ill effects from being preyed upon, but see Stage H3.
c. Omnivores.
If any player except the dog-face player makes a trophic change, he must shift every population tent of the changing genotype into either carnivory or herbivory. Only the dog-faces (mmm) have the omnivore ability, which permits leaving part of a genotype population in a predator triangle, and part on biome cards or husker triangles.
Omnivore tents (dog-face player) are disallowed from preying upon herbivore members of the same genotype. (Cannibalism is thus prohibited, although there is nothing wrong with any player having one of his genotypes eating another of his genotypes.)
D2. Size
Each player may change the size of each of his genotypes one step larger or smaller by rotating its card on the size dial.
a. Limits.
The size may not go beyond the size range listed on each genotype card.
b. DNA recession.
If the size goes beyond the range listed on a DNA card used by that genotype, remove the DNA tent from the genotype card. If the card is being used by another genotype, move the card to that genotype's column even if the slot is already full. If no other genotype is using the card, it is recessed per Stage C2.
Stage E. Population Expansion
In every biome or triangle where each genotype already has one or more tents, a player may add one more population tent of that genotype. Each genotype is limited to the 14 tents provided. Tents may be added to slots in excess of biomass capacity; culling of excess population will not occur until Stage H.
Example: The dog-face player has two archetype tents in the predator triangle of the Sierra Madre, plus two squirrels in the east biome, plus a moose and a squirrel in the west biome. He adds another tent to each of these four populations.
Stage F. Herbivore Migrations
F1. Herbivore Migration
Each herbivore may move to the neighboring slot in its hex, or a number of hexes or borderlands no greater than its range to reach a new biome. A herbivore can occupy either biome slot when it enters a hex.
Migration Limitations
a.
The range of a size 1 or 2 animal is one hex or borderland (or more if equipped with special DNA). Size 3 or larger animals range up to two hexes or borderlands.
b.
Wings permit a range of three hexes or borderlands.
c.
Animals with more than one M DNA may only enter hexes containing at least one flooded slot or marine biome.
d.
Wings or at least one M DNA (amphibians) are required to enter a hex when both slots are marine or flooded. Wings or more than one M DNA are required to cross a flooded bridge. (Animals with exactly one M DNA are termed amphibians, and are able to enter both terrestrial and marine biomes. Genotypes with no M DNA cannot go into all sea hexes or the Atlantic, and genotypes with more than one M DNA cannot leave marine biomes.)
e.
Animals must have wings or at least two P DNA to move through or fly over hexes containing ice-sheets.
f.
Displacement arrows are used for biome displacement, and for all movement in and out of borderlands. Animal migrations from one hex to another are NOT limited by displacement arrows.
F2. Biome Suitability
A herbivore may not end its move on a biome unless it meets all the DNA requirements listed on the biome card. Herbivores may move to slots in excess of biomass capacity; culling of excess population will not occur until Stage H. Multiple genotypes will peacefully coexist in a biome or triangle as long as the biomass can support all the animals.
F3. Borderland Biomes
Herbivores may only enter borderlands along displacement arrows. A herbivore entering a borderland that contains no other herbivores can select any suitable biome to move to the top of the stack (if more than one is present). The top biome is the only one active, the others are kept beneath.
Immigrant herbivores that follow their biome into a borderland lock their biome on top. Should more than one immigrant end up in a borderland, the higher climax biome goes on top, other immigrants go extinct.
Example: After a "Greenhouse down" event, two biomes displace south into the Atlantic rift. Beak lizard, migrating first, moves a herbivore tent there, and chooses his favorite (snails, yum!) to be on top.
F4. Huskers
Each hex may have one or two husker triangles, beneath any biome with the mast acorn icon. Huskers may migrate to these areas as new biome slots with a requirement of H and a niche as stated on the biome card they are underneath. Any genotype moving to a husker triangle during herbivore migrations (Stage F) is a husker.
Example: An oak biome at biomass three can support three herbivores plus three huskers, plus up to six predators eating the plant eaters. Note: mast biomass does not count towards the general hex capacity of four.
Stage G. Carnivore Migrations and Final Size
G1. Predator Migration
Predators move a number of hexes or borderlands no greater than their range to reach a new predator triangle. The migration limitations shown in Stage F1 apply to predator migration.
Tip (predator's dilemma): It is advised that predators go only to places where all the herbivores have prey suitability. If predators move to an overpopulated hex with a mix of roadrunners and non-roadrunners, the non-roadrunners will be culled during Stage H2, and the predators will starve during Stage I.
G2. Final Predator Size Adjust
Predators may change their size by one step.
Example: A predator may wish to migrate two hexes while at size three, then shrink to size two to match the size two herbivore population there.
Note: Due to the omnivore rules, a genotype of the dog-faced cynodont player may have both carnivore and herbivore representatives. However, a dog-faced genotype may change size during Phase G only if all its members are carnivores (no herbivores).
Stage H. Herbivore Cull
Each biome can support a maximum number of herbivore tents equal to its biomass. If a biome has more herbivore tents than its biomass, apply the rules below to identify the losers. Remove losers one at a time until the excess are gone. In case of a tie, go to the next rule.
H1. Niche
Each biome card has niche DNA listed in each of its corners. The herbivores with the least amount of niche DNA lose. If the niche is "SIZE", the smallest herbivores lose.
H2. Roadrunner
Herbivores edible by any predator in the triangle lose. Use prey suitability (below) to determine this.
Prey Suitability.
Each herbivore tent may support one carnivore tent. In order to be able to utilize an intended prey, a predator must:
a. Size.
Be at the same size or not more than two levels smaller than its intended prey. (If the predator is smaller than its prey, it is assumed to be eating eggs or young. Therefore, "Child Care" DNA limits predators to being only the same size as the prey.)
b. Speed.
Have the same number or more "S" DNA than its intended prey.
c. Nocturnal.
Have the same number or more "N" DNA than its intended prey.
d. Armor.
Have the same number or more "A" DNA than its intended prey has "a" DNA.
e. Omnivore cannibalism.
Not prey on members of its own genotype.
H3. Game Warden
If there is only one predator type in the predator triangle which is able to prey on all the herbivores present in both slots plus both husker triangles, then that predator chooses who loses.
Note (Shore Feeding): Predators do not need any 'M' DNA to feed upon herbivores in a marine biome (unless the predators are blocked from entering the hex by migration limitations). Predators with more than one M DNA may feed upon herbivores in a terrestrial biome (again, unless the predators are blocked from entering the hex by migration limitations).
H4. Dentition
Herbivores with the fewest teeth lose. Thus, mmrrr wins over mmm. In case of ties in dentition, the owning player chooses the losers. If all population of one genotype is lost, see extinction (below). If a player loses all his genotypes, see hopeful monster below.
Example: A prairie has a biomass of one but has two herbivores, a moose and an ostrich. In prairies with niche "S" it is the fastest animal that wins, so that an ostrich with two "S" DNA would remain and a moose with only one or none would be removed. If neither have any "S" DNA, go to the next tie-breaker (roadrunner). A lion predator able to eat both moose and ostriches may decide to kill off the ostrich and leave the moose.
Extinction.
The death of the last population tent of a genotype is termed "extinction". Its genotype card is discarded, along with any DNA cards in its column on the Physiology Sheet. If any of its DNA is being used by genotypes that inherited it from the now extinct parent, transfer the DNA card to the existing genotype. This is permitted even if there is no room on the Physiology Sheet for the transferring DNA card. If the player's archetype goes extinct, he may designate another of his genotypes to be the new archetype by swapping tents.
Hopeful Monster.
Any evolutionary freak unable to compete unless an extraterrestrial disaster smites the dominant population. A player's whose last genotype dies may still play on, hoping for an opportunity to purchase a genotype or immigrant epoch card. (A remnant "Lazarus" population is assumed to exist.)
a.
Such players pay one gene at the end of each turn (due to a kind of genetic drift). Players cease paying one gene per turn the turn that they are able to re-establish themselves by purchasing a genotype or immigrant card.
b.
If such a player buys and expresses a genotype card, he may place a single tent anywhere on the map.
c.
Such players are eligible to bid on an immigrant card and express it as if it were a genotype. If expressed, treat the first tent as if it was an invading immigrant, but the player can choose what DNA to select for his "W". A player who re-enters the game in this manner may never bid on any other genotype or immigrant later in the game; he may only bid on a DNA for his genotype. The immigrant uses all genotype rules for the remainder of the game with a 14 tent limit. Players who purchase an immigrant card use the dentition printed on its card for all purposes except if they are tied during herbivore or carnivore cull. This can happen if they are competing against another immigrant, for instance. In such cases, the player's original dentition is used to break the tie.
Stage I. Carnivore Cull
Each predator tent chooses a suitable prey tent. Predators without prey are removed. Order of choice is by:
I1. Size
Larger predators choose before smaller predators.
I2. Dentition
Predators with fewer teeth choose before those with more teeth.
Note: Prey suitability (H2), extinction (H4) and hopeful monster rules (H4) also apply to this stage.
Ending the Game
The game ends at the end of the turn during which one of the following happens:
a.
The last epoch card is played (i.e., the Pleistocene/Holocene card).
b.
All players agree to halt.
c.
Only one player (or none) has population tents on the map.
d.
A predetermined time limit is reached.
e.
The Greenhouse goes over 600 ppm or under 150 ppm.
f. Blitzkrieg (optional):
If immigrant Hominids [#140] enter the game during the Cenozoic, and are not held up by a closed bridge from Asia.
Determining the Winner.
The winner is the player with the highest cumulative population (counting the tents of all genotypes on the map). Count the population:
a.
At the end of the last Mesozoic Era turn (not counted if playing the 12-turn game)
b.
At the end of the game (see above).
The player with the highest total wins. If tied, the player with the greatest number of genes wins.
Optional Games
a. Classroom Game.
Students are divided into teams; each team has one archetype. As each archetype speciates into new forms, individual team members are assigned control of these orders.
Note: Permission is granted to photocopy additional Physiology Sheets for classroom use. Smaller copies of the map are available from Sierra Madre Games for classroom use, so that each team may have their own map.
b. Darwinian Game.
Predators lose the ability to be game wardens. Instead dentition resolves such herbivore ties. In addition, if there are three or four players, the mmm and rrrr starting archetypes receive a "P" and an "A" DNA respectively. Players should put these DNA tents sideways to show they are heritage and thus not inheritable by future genotypes.
c. Poikilothermic Reptiles.
Both reptile players are assumed to start cold blooded. The following rules apply to all "cold blooded" players:
Cold-bloods cannot express N, S, wings, or sex DNA, and ignore N, S, or wings heritage DNA.
Double biome biomass capacity for cold-blood herbivores. Thus, each point of biomass may support two cold-bloods. (Size 1 cold-bloods have one-fifth the calorie requirements of homoiotherms.)
Allow two cold-blood predators per herbivore tent.
Maximum migration range of 1 regardless of size.
Not allowed adjacent to a hex with an ice-sheet.
All reptile and insect genotypes and immigrants are cold-blooded until they attain more than one P DNA.
Example of Play
Two-Player Advanced Game
This example shows the first few game turns of a game of American Megafauna. The reptile player is the dino-croc (rm) and the mammal player is the dog-face (mmm). Numbers in [ ] are card numbers.
Setup.
The dino-croc puts a population tent in the ginkgoes of Laurentian Shield, and so does the dog-face.
Card One [#54].
First card is number 54, DNA MM. Since this card restricts its user to marine biomes, neither player bids on it. (Had a player expressed this card, he would have had to move to the Epeiric and eat primitive fish.) Both players grow to size two, multiply their population to two apiece, and scatter from the Laurentian ginkgoes to adjacent hexes (Greenland, Spain, Appalachia).
Card Two [#111].
Turtle genotype. Each player bids one gene, then escalates to three apiece. The reptile wins this tie (having more r teeth per the Greenhouse Level). He expresses it: a turtle replaces a dino-croc in Spain, and Milankovich P causes a drought and a bloom. All of the vegetation in the tropics, plus the rainforest in the shield, shrink to one as the Earth dries out. The bloom raises the tropical plants back up to two, plus expands the sand dunes to three. The cloud forest in Appalachia shrinks to one as the dunes expand. The dog-face now has four tents; the reptile also has four, half dino-croc and half tortoises. All populations are herbivorous and size three.
Card Three [#66].
Tool Use W DNA. Neither player is interested in this card, as it cannot be expressed without first acquiring other cards, which may never come up. It seems likely that, if bought, this card will fall to genetic drift, yet the reptile decides it may be worth one gene and the mammal fatally lets him have it. During Stage D, the dino-croc archetypes switch to carnivore. The turtles, with 'Iaa' DNA, will soon crowd out the dog-faces in the sites that have a "Niche 'I'" (cloud forest, the rainforest, and Spanish bog.)
Card Four [#73].
Antlers 'sex' DNA! Obviously both players lust after this card, so the mammal puts one gene on it instantly. Since the reptile has but one gene left and ties on antlers go to the mammal, the dog-face is quickly adorned with a proud rack. It seems that the game is lost for the reptile. However, his turtles spread into ginkgoes and Greenland ferns. The turtles win because they are double-armored roadrunners inedible to the local dino-croc predators, who can only eat dog-faces. In desperation, dog-face grows to size five and moves to the two biomes with "Niche: size". These two biomes, seed ferns [#168] in Talkeetna, and lycopod meadows [#171] in the Sierra Madre, are havens for the dog-face, since the rival turtles are limited to size four.
Card Five [#144].
Choristoderans invade over the Cocos bridge. These enigmatic lizard-like predators have DNA of MW, and a size range of 1-4. They first try to eat the size five dog-faces in the meadows of the Sierra Madre. The native dino-croc predators are still at size 3, but they can grow twice during the turn to size five. This is big enough to crush the size four invader godzillas. Size matters!
Card Six [#99].
Thumb Spike 'Ia' DNA. Disaster for the mammal as he has no genes and the reptile picks this up for a gene. Not only does the thumb spike have great DNA, but it also happens to be the card allowing him to express the tool-use (W) card in his hand. The reptile gives his predators thumbs, but is too heavy to express his tool-use just yet. He hopes his clever predators will drive the dog-faces extinct before they can exploit their sex advantage. Population at this point: Dog-face: 4 (eating seed ferns and lycopod meadows); dino-crocs: 4 (eating dog-faces), and ninja turtles: 8. The Triassic is now over; the Jurassic begins.
Card Seven [#158].
Digging claws NI DNA. The mammals buy this, and the dog-faces go nocturnal, hoping to drive the last two predators extinct. But the dino-croc cheerfully announces that he is inventing the torch, expressing his tool use W DNA as "N".
Card Eight [#47].
Two bridges flood, a mild "catastrophe". The mammal gets ten genes, the reptile but five. The mammal is rich but close to extinction, only four population tents on the two "Niche: size" biomes. But they are at size five and have more teeth. His opponent decides to go for broke to kill off these last four, growing to size four, and switches to vegetarianism. He displaces turtles so his newly herbivorous dinosaurs have a home. His daring plan is to grow big enough to knock the mammal herbivores out of their size-niche biomes and out of the game. His tool-use must stay recessed because the dino-crocs are now too big, so his plan risks losing it to genetic drift!
Card Nine [#114].
The mammal desperately buys the dolphin genotype, eating primitive fish in the Epeiric seaway (which induces the reptile to abandon his risky extirpation effort).
By the end of the Jurassic, the mammal gets deer and camels, which turn out to be poor investments. On turn 23 (mid-Cretaceous), the dinosaurs get technology (!!), and invent fishing poles (fishing for both dolphins and rays in the Gulf). The techno-predators, now with bows and arrows, kill off the dolphins and camels. The dog-faces barely hang on as huge generalized predators feeding on generalized prey, and the deer are forced into Europe and survive only by being triple speed and nocturnal. The turtles remain inedible, even to the now humanoid dinosaurs. Toothed birds and Pachycephalosaurs come but don't live long.
Final Populations (Sum of K/T and Quaternary Stages):
Reptile: turtles: 28, techno-dinosauroids: 18, brontosaurs: 18
Mammal: dog-face: 8, swift nightdeer: 6, giant tillodont rats: 8
Victory: Ruling reptiles!
Ecological Lessons
Tips on How to Play and Win
Grow a Nice Tree.
Think of your archetype as the root to a nicely rounded tree that you want to build, and buy cards accordingly. Genotypes are like main branches, DNA are like leaves. Buy some DNA early so that other genotypes have something to inherit, but don't overdo it or you'll over-specialize (see next tip). Genotypes are more valuable than DNA cards because they offer the most flexibility and DNA. But don't buy only genotypes or else you'll have tree limbs without leaves and it will cost you more to fit them all out with DNA without free inheritance. Ideally, by the end of the game, you should have four creatures with some minimum shared DNA, but all looking substantially different and none over-specialized.
Don't Get Greedy (or, the "Bolide Bash").
An over-specialized creature is one which is so loaded with DNA that you can barely read the card. Such a creature has a lot of ability to survive, but the penalty for such super-specialization is the ultimate one — outright termination! — if the proper Catastrophe should drop in. There is an 18% chance that sometime during the Mesozoic any creature with heritage AND purchased DNA of more than three of any of A, a, B, G, H, M, or S and/or triple S dies from "comet smite"! During the Cenozoic, an asteroid kills anything with more than two of A, a, M, or S and, or triple B (the "Roadrunner Crusher"). Another Cenozoic catastrophe (supernova) kills those having more than four of A, a, G, H, I, N, and S and/or having triple I. Yet another (solar flare) kills anything bigger than size 4, or with more than two A or a. Horned dinosaurs are vulnerable to comets and the brontosaurs will die in either an asteroid or a solar flare. You may choose to live dangerously, but we recommend waiting until the Cenozoic when the chances of catastrophe decrease (to a 15% risk of flare or asteroid).
Don't Overspend.
Just like super-specialization, the penalties for overspending can be severe. If you have the fewest genes, there are three pandemics (one in the Mesozoic and two in the Cenozoic) which force you to kill off a genotype. Even worse, having few genes means your opponent can pick up cards for next to nothing. When bidding you should always examine the card not only from your point of view, but from that of the other player. What will he do with such a card and how much will that be worth to him? Will it let him add just two tents or is it more like 10? How high is the climax of the biomes in question? Knowing this should tell you something about how much he intends to bid and you can bid up accordingly. If he doesn't see it and you don't need the card, you may want to point out its value to him! Even if you don't need the card, you need to make sure your opponent pays full value or else buy it yourself and just hold it.
The "Roadrunner Effect."
Roadrunner cards hurt predators but only temporarily hurt your herbivore competitors (see next tip). In the meantime you have wasted precious genes perhaps better spent on another B or an H which will help you survive fluctuating conditions. What DNA cards should herbivores buy? The approximate value of DNA early in the game are H, wings, and sex (8 genes), B and G (7 genes), M (6), I (3), and N and P (2). By the Cenozoic, it is likely that flowers, grasses, and deciduous trees will have appeared, making G, H, and P DNA more valuable.
Predator's Dilemma.
The top predator controls the game (in "game warden" fashion), and as such he should try to buy roadrunner DNA and genotypes that come up. But what if a herbivore gets one, say a turtle genotype, which the carnivore cannot eat? Suddenly, wherever the turtle goes, there is a metastable situation: if the predator stays, the animal he cannot eat proliferates, and if he goes away, the native herbivore that he can eat wins (only to disappear if the predator returns and turtles are still around). Unlike the cartoons, a carnivore must retreat from a roadrunner, in order to isolate its spread. If there is a refuge (like a biome where an edible herbivore has a niche that the turtle does not have), the carnivore should go there. Barring this, he may have to switch to being a herbivore.
The 60 Ton Wiener Dog.
Gratuitous size changes may inconvenience a predator, but you may find yourself unable to adapt in time to a changing situation. For example, suppose your biome changes out from under you. You need to play a new DNA card to adapt, but you can't because it is limited to sizes 2-4 and you're at size 6. It takes 2 turns to change size, but you have only one, so you're dead (or at least at risk to losing your investment to genetic drift, which has a 14% chance of occurring every turn). Size three is about ideal for migration range, and expression of DNA.
The Streak.
An interesting technique is to buy Speed for your archetype then spawn off a genotype which may well be the only creature which can predate on it. As long as you can keep the most S on your herbivore and predator, or keep all the S out of other player's hands, you can double your population. This tip is much easier to accomplish with Speed or Nocturnal than with Armor/Anti-armor. Warning: See "Don't Get Greedy" for the dangers of being a super-roadrunner.
Sex and Death.
If you can get it early enough (during the Early Cretaceous at the latest), the Sex DNA is worth about eight genes, but probably no more. In general, it is more important to watch the ratios between gene pool totals than the subtractive difference. For example, the difference between your having 11 genes and the other player having 16 (ratio of 16:11 or about 1.45) is a lot less serious than your having one and his having six (ratio 6:1). Unless something happens, in the latter case you automatically lose the next four auctions whereas in the former, after one or two auctions, parity will return.
Lost World.
Keep an archetype in Spain in case it floats out to sea and becomes a haven from the continental lifeforms.
Dinosaur Mysteries.
Perhaps while playing this game, you can figure out the three fundamental dinosaur mysteries:
a.
Dinosaurs are big. Reptiles, birds, and mammals developed tiny species, but never dinosaurs.
b.
Dinosaurs are terrestrial. Reptiles, birds, and mammals have had marine and flying forms. Not dinosaurs.
c.
Dinosaurs are dead. Reptiles, birds, and mammals all had survivors. Dinosaurs didn't.
Milieu
The Game of Big Game
a. The Big Phenotypes.
I am not talking lions and tigers and bears big. A Manchurian Tiger weighs in at 325 kg; that's only size two in this game. Hippos, at 1300 kg, are only size three. America has seen far bigger animals than this. Brachiosaurs mass 70 tons, perhaps twice that for the Ultrasaurus species.
b. Size Matters.
Studies have shown that megafauna gain a disproportionate share of the resources in an area. Large animals are often faster, migrate further, and can catch bigger prey than small ones. Weight per weight, megafauna need less food than small animals and do not need a high metabolism for the same activity level. Finally, large animals have smaller populations than small ones, given a constant resource. Smaller populations exhibit more speciation, at the price of greater genetic drift.
c. Big Warm-bloods.
Another advantage of being huge is having a surface area to volume ratio favorable to high metabolism warm-bloodedness. One can visualize this principle by observing how fast a given amount of ice melts in cubes compared to if it is in one big block. Tiny homoiotherms like shrews and hummingbirds must eat continuously to maintain their body heat.
d. No American Megafauna?
In all the world, America has had the most and the biggest dinosaurs by far. Yet although the conifer forests are still here, Brachiosaurs no longer roam in them. This continent is impoverished in big game, compared both to its past and to modern Africa.
e. Leaves.
By far the most abundant terrestrial food is low-energy leaves, watery and full of fiber. Only 30 to 70% of the contained energy of leaves is usable, compared to 70 to 90% for high energy foods (fruit, rodents, insects, fish, and seeds). The smallest leaf eating warm-bloods are the rabbits and sage grouses (these uncommon American birds have a diet that is 71% leaves and flowers of the sagebrush). Smaller homoiotherms will eat some leaves, but would starve without high energy insects, seeds, or meat.
f. Guts.
Most of the energy of a leaf is locked up in cellulose, the most common component of fiber. No known animal can digest fiber by itself; leaf-eating animals must employ long digestive tracts filled with special bacteria in order to extract energy from cellulose. Vegetation may be processed mainly in the foregut (stomach or crop) or hindgut (colon or intestine). In today's biomes, midsize herbivores tend to be foregut digesters like ruminants, while the larger herbivores (elephants, rhinos, hippos, horses, and extinct sloths, ankylosaurs, and pachycephalosaurs) use hindgut fermentation. A large amount of foliage can be processed rapidly in the hindgut, but the droppings will contain much undigested food. Elephants spend 77% of their time eating because of digestive inefficiency.
g. Grass.
The warm, wet forests were largely replaced by the cool, dry grasslands during the Miocene. This cooling was the result of the rise of the Tibetan plateau leaching greenhouse gases on a planetary scale. Grass is particularly hard to digest. Cattle, deer, and other ruminants are today's most advanced grazers; such cud-chewers are currently driving the larger hindgut digesters out. Rhinos, elephants, and horses will be extinct in a million years or so unless man gives them sanctuary.
h. Predator Size.
Africa statistics show that mammal predators (wild dogs, cheetah, leopards, lions) rarely catch prey more than twice their mass. Although a pride of lions (150 kg each) can bring down a 420 kg buffalo, herbivores over a ton (elephants, rhinos, hippos, giraffes) are immune to predators.
i. Flight.
Wings are perhaps the strongest card in the game. I allow their use on size two animals (80-400 kg). Debatable, since the heaviest known flyers are two American animals: the pterodactyl (Quetzalcoatlus) and birds (Teratorns, and Argentavis, a condor). These New World flyers span from 7 to 11 meters, and mass perhaps 85 kg. Bustards, today's heaviest flyers, mass 23 kg.
The Prehistoric Contest
a. Permian Period.
The two-tusked dicynodont, a therapsid, was the only known proto-mammal (Tartarian) survivor of the Permian extinctions. During the post-holocaust Synthian radiation, another therapsid emerged, the carnivorous cynodont with a dog-like face. These pre-mammals were quickly threatened as top carnivores by archosaurs such as the pseudosuchian thecodonts. Also on the rise were the beaked rhynchosaurs, close to the origins of modern lizards and snakes.
b. Triassic Period.
The Triassic was a mighty clash of two nascent dynasties. The first, archosaurs, would rule the Mesozoic, and the second, mammals, would rule the Cenozoic. But in the Triassic, at the dawn of the Mesozoic, both dynasties began on equal footing. This is where our game begins.
c. Jurassic Period.
Coastlines are similar to today's. The Triassic extinctions killed the two-tuskers and rhynchosaurs, and the dog-faced cynodonts will not survive the Jurassic. However, one cynodont group evolved into the first true mammal, a small egg-laying shrewish critter. The archosaurs remain supreme, radiating into fantastic forms: dinosaurs, pterosaurs, crocodiles, and birds.
d. Cretaceous Period.
A seaway full of giant marine reptiles divides America into East and West islands, and the world is warmer and cloudier than any age since. The dinosaurs evolve to spectacular heights, then die off under suspicious circumstances. The tiny mammals, reptiles, and birds survive.
e. Tertiary Period.
Mammals rule and dinosaurs drool! Was it inevitable? Had the dinosaurs become so senescent that the first comet to come by would take them out, and leave the mice to rule? I think not, or I would not have designed this game.
f. Quaternary Period.
The contest is not yet over. The archosaurs live on as birds, whose scaly bipedal feet belie their dinosaurian past, eager for any chance to regain their status as terrestrial superlative.
Implications of American Megafauna
Like all Sierra Madre games, American Megafauna is marketed as an educational game, worthy of didactic exercises in biology classrooms. And so I have had occasion to explain its premises to faith-bound teachers and parents. Most acknowledge a place for evolution, at least as an explanation for the development of the various human races from postulated original Adam and Eve archetypes, for instance. Most will admit that, if placed on a jury in a paternity trial, they would accept DNA evidence as "proof beyond a reasonable doubt" that a deadbeat dad was related to a child. Given that chimpanzees share 95% of their DNA with their human relatives, the idea of humans as just another type of animal is beyond controversy. Within the context of current knowledge, evolution is a fact.
Irreconcilable differences.
The differences between science and religion are not over evidence, but are rather epistemological. I find these differences irreconcilable. Science asserts that all knowledge comes from observations and reason, and all phenomena are causal and natural (and thus knowable), while the deists assert that knowledge is faith-based, and hold out for supernatural (and thus unknowable) portions of existence. There is no room for compromise between these two views, despite many claims to the contrary. One can either have a natural universe run by natural "knowable" laws, or one can have a universe run by a willful supernatural agency whose nature and intentions are, by definition of the word "supernatural", both unknown and unknowable. A lawful universe that contains even a single "lawless" poltergeist is, in principle, indistinguishable from an utterly chaotic Byronic universe. How can one tell if any observation or experiment was the result of principle or poltergeist? If the supernatural exists in any degree, then the efficaciousness of science is utterly illusionary.
The nature of proof.
My rant is not so much against the deists, but against the would-be defenders of science that seek compromise between science and religion, often by softening the definition of "proof". As an adherent of the Objectivist philosophy, I find such elasticity to be fatal to the very nature of objectivity. Any hypothesis is asserted within a context that may be strengthened or weakened as new evidence comes in. If favorable evidence continues to be discovered, with no contraries, a cognitive climax is reached. The conclusion ceases to be a hypothesis, and becomes knowledge. Certain knowledge. Despite the assertions of modern philosophers such as Karl Popper, certainty and proof are possible, within the context of one's knowledge.
Evolution's metronome.
The evolutionary scale of this game encompasses animal orders, rather than species. The pace of change of families, genera, and species is too frenetic to simulate in a 6 My (million year) game turn. Fossils indicate that the mean duration of a genus is about 20 My, and that of a species is just 4 My. There are many examples of vertebrate populations that have, in the course of human lifetimes, been formed with characteristics distinctive enough that they no longer interbreed. Speciation evolution has been observed directly, in Darwinian finches, sockeye salmon (stream-bred versus lake-bred varieties), Australian rabbit immigrants, scarlet honeycreepers (shorter beaks in response to loss of its favorite flower in Hawaii), littorine snails (armor in response to crab predation), and novel species of mosquitofish. During the Triassic, when the world was one big Gondwanaland, the pace of change was stale. Today, the intermingling of species from seven continents may constitute the greatest speciation event since the Cambrian explosion.
Die Achterbahn.
Players of American Megafauna get a sense of a white-knuckle ride in a planetary Achterbahn (the German word for roller coaster). The actual pace of events is even faster than the simulation provides. During a single game turn of 6 million years (6 My), target Earth can expect to be pelted with a rock big enough to leave a scar 50 km in diameter, sufficient to extinguish 20% of the species. The last 6 My has seen about 100 Ice Ages and as many interglacials. Imagine Canada alternately white with glacial ice and green with tropical forests. The survivors of such turmoil are resilient and adaptable, and not the frail things depicted commonly by environmental mystics.
Ground Zero at the K-T Catastrophe.
A giant bolide 10 km in diameter can be expected to smite the Earth every 200 million years (My). The last one left a scar 170 km in diameter in the Yucatán peninsula. The rebounding crust registered an estimated 13 on the Richter scale, and generated world-wide tsunamis a hundred meters tall. The re-entry rain of ejecta literally ignited the lower atmosphere, burning everything combustible. A curtain of nitrogen oxide smog and soot shut down photosynthesis for months, and destroyed the ozone layer. The gloom plunged Earth into a decades-long impact winter, with average temperatures below freezing. Since ground zero happened to be rich in sulfur, acid rain sulfates pelted the world for decades. The surviving plants and animals seemed to be acid-tolerating or else living on acid-buffering soils. And the already high greenhouse was elevated for at least a millennium. Of the three "mass-extinction" calamities that have occurred during the 250 My game period, this was the mildest. Another mass extinction ended the Triassic, killing 76% of the species. But the worst was the Permian event, which killed off 96% of the world's plants and animals. Mercifully, the four or five catastrophes expected during a typical American Megafauna game only kill off 15% or less of the player genotypes.
The mother of all extinctions.
It is now known that the biggest event ever, the Permian disaster that is the occasion for the start of the game, was as sudden as it was apocalyptic. Both land and sea were affected. The Mesozoic life that populated the post-holocaust world was so utterly different from the Paleozoic oceans that some early paleontologists had wondered if life had entirely died out and been reborn. The deep oceans became hypoxic, a sign of high global temperatures, and sea levels fluctuated. Sedimentology records show that rivers worldwide became braided as the denuded land choked them with sediment. Photosynthesis shut down. Coal formation abruptly stopped, replaced by "redbeds", sandstone deposits indicative of warm arid regions. In many ways, the signature is similar to the K-T event, known to be impact-caused. But where are the blankets of debris, or the notorious tell-tale layer of iridium? Another difference is that the Permian event dumped enormous amounts of organic carbon into the atmosphere and oceans, far more than can be explained by the death of all the biota on the planet. This huge spike in the carbon cycle might be explained by massive methane releases from the continental shelves in the face of dropping sea levels. The sudden appearance of titanic flood basalts in a geologically quiet region of Siberia is also suggestive. There are no indications that the Siberian traps were impact-related, and so the mystery lingers.
A painful recovery.
Life rebounded rapidly, within a geologically-brief hundred thousand years, for most of the mass extinctions. Not so the mother of all extinctions. Compared to the riotous diversity of the late Permian rocks, the sandstones of the Early Triassic are monotonously barren the world over. For several million years marine fossils show nothing but a smattering of cosmopolitan weed species, especially small scallops or brachiopods adapted for low oxygen waters. Terrestrial plants were also exclusively sporadic weedy opportunists, consisting of spiky quillworts, shrubby lycopods, a seed fern, horsetails, and some small conifers. There are no signs of trees until newly-evolved conifer forests make an appearance 5 My after the event. At about this time, more or less the game's starting point, the first megafauna appear: an array of hopeful monsters. However, even 15 My after the event, megafauna populations were still depauperate, consisting mainly of a few scattered species of world-wide distribution, notably the two-tusker Lystrosaurus.
Biodiversity trends.
Despite such gyrations of the Achterbahn, or rather because of them, biodiversity on the planet is at an all time high. The number of marine families seems to have plateaued during the Paleozoic at about 500, before the Permian crash. But after the painful recovery, biodiversity steadily rose during the Mesozoic to 700 families, with a hiccup at the K-T boundary of a hundred families or so, before resuming the climb to 900 families today. The number of terrestrial families vacillated greatly around 200 families through the Paleozoic and Mesozoic eras, but exploded after the K-T event to the 800 families in recent fossil records.
Two recent invasions of America.
America has seen two extinction events in the last 6 million year game turn. Both were paleozoogeographic invasions. The most recent was the megafauna extinctions of the late Pleistocene, 11,000 years ago. About 35 genera were extirpated, including all the proboscideans (mastodons, gomphotheres, mammoths), seven genera of giant ground sloths, two genera of xenothera (a glyptodont and an armadillo), three genera of horses (at least 10 species), two species of tapirs, two genera of peccaries, a camel and two llamas, a mountain deer, the elk-moose, three types of pronghorns, the saiga, three oxen (yak, shrub, and musk), three cats (sabertooth, scimitartooth, and cheetah), three bears (spectacled, short-faced, and cave), two skunks, the giant beaver, the dire wolf, and the last of the litopterns and notoungulates. Perhaps 73% of the megafauna died off or were driven to refuges elsewhere. According to Dr. Paul Martin's blitzkrieg hypothesis, these unfortunate creatures were inexperienced in the ways of men and their spears coming over the Kula Bridge. These megafauna losses are comparable to the losses (46 genera) that occurred during another bridge invasion three million years earlier. The long-flooded Cocos Bridge opened in Panama, allowing mammal invasions both north and south. The southbound invaders, placental mammals, were the most destructive, and South America lost most of her neotropical marsupials and unique ungulates in this exchange, formally called the Great American Interchange.
Western Civilization arrives.
Compared with these invasions, the arrival of Columbus and the Industrial Revolution has had scant ecological impact. No genera have gone extinct, and the known species of plants and animals that have died off are under a dozen. From the mammal megadynasty, one Mexican rabbit is gone from the continent, as well as the Stellar's Sea Cow offshore. From the dinosaur megadynasty, out of 750 extant nearctic bird species, the casualties include the Passenger pigeon, Carolina parakeet, the Labrador duck, and the Great auk. This death rate is well below the expected "natural" rate of vertebrate extinctions.
Modern mass extinction?
It is often alleged that we are in the middle of a mass extinction event today. Balderdash. World-wide, about 113 bird and 83 mammal species known to have gone extinct since 1600, and almost all these are marginal island species such as the dodo. Such species, having evolved in the absence of predators, are complacent and naïve, doomed the moment a piece of driftwood or continental drift brings the first predator ashore. Subtract the island species, and the subspecies, and the observed extinction rate is just about zero. Far from disappearing, the 9800 bird and 4600 mammal species appear to be radiating into new species at many times faster than they are going extinct. Compare this to a real mass extinction, such as the aftermath of the K-T rockfall. Some 70% of the globe's species, including the dinosaurs, were suddenly snuffed out. Given the fecundity of life, where a single gravid female can repopulate a species, it is estimated that this event would have had to have killed 99.99% of all individuals of all species to end the legacy of three quarters of the species. In contrast, the biomass of today's planet is more or less stable.
Man as Game Warden.
Unlike other species, humans tend to conserve species and environments they find desirable. For example, many hindgut-digesting megafauna, such as Indian elephants and horses, have long been in decline from competition from more advanced ruminant mammals such as deer and cattle. These animals would likely be extinct today, if it were not for man's intervention. Other critters that have benefited from man's domestication efforts are cheetahs, camels, oxen, donkeys, hamsters, guinea pigs, and ginkgo trees. DNA studies suggest that every "wild" representative of these species today is descended from domesticated captives of several thousand years ago. Ancient "bio-engineering" has also increased biodiversity by producing hundreds of new varieties of plants and animals, from maize to cattle. Human habitat modification, from the creation of fields and open woodlands to the erection of cities, usually increase rather than decrease both the numbers and types of wildlife in a given region. One reason is that animals like open woodlands better than closed forests. Another reason is the human introduction of exotic immigrants, both deliberate introductions and accidental hitchhikers. The introduction of a developed area into a wilderness increases diversity about 10% in the long run (40% in island communities). Such human-caused increases in biodiversity and biome-diversity also increase the conditions for speciation events.
Climate changes as natural cycles.
The impact of man upon the Achterbahn has been insignificant so far. Contrary to the headlines, the role of nature is far larger than man. A single large volcano or hurricane delivers more chlorine compounds to the atmosphere than man's entire history of CFC production. The eruptions of acidic volcanoes such as El Chicón in Mexico have been linked to most of the world's acid rain. Most sulfur dioxide emissions are also volcanic, 147 million tons from the Laki volcanic eruption in Iceland alone. Most methane comes from wetlands, ruminants, and termites. Forests produce more than six times more hydrocarbons than all man-made sources. Carbon dioxide levels today, 380ppm, are 99% from natural sources and are far less than they were in the Eocene and Cretaceous (over 1125 ppm). The forests of North America, which have been expanding for a century, absorb more CO2 than the continent's industry produces, making America a net greenhouse sink. Today's global warming is part of a natural cycle, one of a dozen interglacial episodes in the last million years. The world warmed rapidly starting 18,000 years ago, 7°C per century at its peak, and slowing irregularly to today's half degree per millennium. I mention these vignettes to give an idea of the scale of the catastrophe cards, compared with the influences of modern humans.
Agriculture as the biggest human impact on land.
When speaking of ecological impact, sometimes measured as an ecological "footprint", many imagine cities, highways, and sprawl. However, developed land, where 75% of Americans live, takes up only 27 million hectares (Mha), less than 3% of the land area. The biggest footprint associated with humanity for the last 10,000 years has been agriculture. Cropland in the U.S. takes up some 141 Mha, more than three quarters of it to produce fodder for cattle. (For comparison, a hex in American Megafauna is about 180 Mha.) This agricultural footprint is about a quarter of the area of flood basalt volcanism and glacial incursions that America has endured the last 6 million years.
Technology decreases one's ecological footprint.
It requires 2800 hectares (Ha) to support each person of hunter-gatherer technology. The American continent could support less than a million such persons, and yet their impact was sufficient to devastate the megafauna. "Given their technology, the Indians had probably reached the numbers that represented the carrying capacity of the land. All of the area of the present United States that was habitable at all was occupied by some tribe or tribes; there were no empty lands." The technology of animal domestication shrank this footprint by a factor of 50; in other words, it requires only 56 Ha to support each wandering herdsman. Slash-burn technology requires only 11 Ha per person, and short-fallow agriculture is down to 2 Ha. The Industrial Revolution halved this so that by 1866 half the population of the United States was freed up for non-agricultural purposes. It required 1.35 Ha to produce a tonne of wheat, comparable to sub-Saharan Africa today. The Green revolution reduced the footprint by another factor of four, down to 0.22 Ha per person. Replacing draft animals with tractors also drastically reduced the amount of land needed, because of the fodder acreage saved. Moreover, the number of man-hours to produce this tonne of wheat has improved five thousand times since 1866, such that less than 1% of the population now makes the U.S. the world's greatest food exporter. The resulting food glut from high yield farms has caused the abandonment of many former croplands, which are often reclaimed by forests. Indeed, the woodlands of America have been expanding consistently for a century, to an area today twice that of croplands. The technology of best practices in the U.S., such as phytofarms, hydroponics, and aquaculture, has footprints at least 2000 times smaller still. Innovations have reduced the per capita environmental impact at a rate many times that of the population growth rate.
Technology increases resources.
Like all creatures, man alters its environment to suit its needs. But whereas animals only consume resources that nature provides, only man can create new resources, through the miracle called ideas and technology. Coincident is the increase of wealth and abundance. For example, the amount of living space per person has increased as the technology of house-building has improved, and as housing prices (compared to wages) have fallen. The last few centuries have seen a steady decrease in the prices (again compared to wages) of metals and all other raw materials as new ideas for locating, extracting, and conserving them have been realized. The worldwide amount of food per capita is also at an all time high for the same reasons. The poorest persons today enjoy sanitation, health, communication, and other benefits denied to the richest pharaoh, the grandest czar, or to the sun king of France. The key idea is that resources are not analogous to a pie baked by mother nature, that one must grab before one's greedy brethren do. Resources are the marriage of sweat and ideas, and American entrepreneurs are creating more resources than any other society in recorded history.
Technology reduces pollution.
A high tech person has less impact on the environment than a low tech one. The richest and most industrialized countries are also the cleanest, since anything that retards economic growth also retards environmental cleanup. Pollution was worse in pre-Columbian America than it is today. The technology of Native Americans was insufficient to give them living areas better than unsanitary and overcrowded smoke-filled rooms, to stop the salinization of their fields, or to prevent their teeth from being worn away by grit in their stone-ground foods. Even today the greatest air pollution health problems are the indoor emissions due to third-world heating and cooking with wood, coal, and dung. Technology reduces waste. An average American household produces a couple of kilos of waste today, less than half that of a century ago (in 1900 homes produced two kilos per day of coal ash alone), and less than in developing nations such as Mexico. Moving outside, pollution and health in the streets of New York improved dramatically when it changed from horses to cars. (As did the smell.) The emissions per capita of carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and particulates increased during the first half of the 20th century, but are today less than half what they were 50 years ago and dropping, and are no longer a health issue in developed nations. Pollution is an old problem, solvable only through technology.
Proper human goals.
These observations are not to foster complacency, but to illustrate how the "deep time" of American Megafauna can give perspective to the real problems facing us today. Warning, another game element, the population and culling rules, should not be applied to humanity. These rules are patterned after the Laws of Malthus, which apply only to non-technological beings. Of all the animals, only humans have the capacity to conceptualize changes, and work towards that vision. The "masses" are commonly held to be stupid ignoble creatures, who would destroy themselves without benevolent paternal oversight. I see zero evidence for this patronizing view, held both by theologians and political leaders left and right. It is true that a proper philosophy is needed to intelligently guide man's emerging control over his surroundings. Beneficial human goals are not those that maintain equilibrium, since this is not possible on the Achterbahn. They are also not those that punish industrialists for the creation of resources and wealth. Modern problems, the exact opposite of those predicted by Malthus, include crippling underpopulation, obesity, statist politics, and mental health issues associated with our new-found longevity. And naturally Ludditism, the fear of technology.

Endnotes
Permian Extinction
, the most severe mass extinction known, in which 96% of species went extinct. This catastrophe swept land and sea, plants and animals. The game starts in the aftermath of this holocaust, and allows the proto-mammals to setup last since they had the initial upper hand, particularly as herbivores. Possible causes of the Permian extinctions include an asteroid strike, or the Siberian Traps.
Pangea.
During the Triassic, at the dawn of this game, all the major continents are locked into a super-continent called Pangea. At 210 million years ago (Ma), rifts appeared as North America and Europe ("Laurasia") split from South American and Africa. Sea water poured into the spreading zones of the Atlantic Rift. At 85 Ma, an extension of the Atlantic would temporarily open between North America and Europe.
Dinosaurs
, an archosaur vertebrate group close to crocodiles but showing birdlike features in the hips and hind legs. This allowed a more vertical limb alignment capable of sprints and gallops compared to the splayed-out limbs typical of reptiles. Dinosaurs likely inherited a warm-blooded oxidative metabolism, free of the lactic acid build-up that bedevils the fermentive metabolism of reptiles. (This may explain why there are no dinosaurs as small as lizards.) Dinosaurs arose from a pseudosuchian thecodont in the Mid to Late Triassic, and went extinct during the K/T event.
The K/T Event
, a massive extinction at or near the Cretaceous-Tertiary boundary (abbreviated K/T) some 66 million years ago. On the land, the dinosaurs and pterosaurs died. In the oceans, the rudistids (coral-like oysters), great sea reptiles and lizards, ammonites, and much plankton went extinct. Small and generalized cold-bloods, like fish, turtles, insects, crocodiles, seemed totally unaffected, as did plants (even the recently evolved angiosperms). Small warm-blooded animals like mammals and birds not only survived but radiated spectacularly. This upheaval, which seemed to plummet the unusually high greenhouse that prevailed through the Late Cretaceous, has been associated with the Chicxulub impact crater in Yucatan. Thus ended the Mesozoic Era.
Sequence of Play
A. Reveal Epoch Card
Biome:
Receive additional genes (sex DNA = 2, otherwise 1). The biome card is placed on map in the legal slot with the lowest climax. Biome is rotated so that the total hex biomass is four. Displaced biomes move to an empty slot one hex away if one exists, preferring southern and western directions.
DNA:
Auctioned, ties to genotype with most r or m teeth as stated on card.
Genotype:
Auctioned, ties to genotype with most r or m teeth as per greenhouse level.
Immigrant:
If drift specified, each player loses one recessed card. If a bridge is flooded, immigrant waits until the bridge clears, otherwise its card is placed on the first suitable biome/triangle, scanning from the south (north for Asian immigrants), then along each row west to east.
Catastrophe:
Receive additional genes (sex DNA/2-player game = 6; sex DNA/3 or more player game = 10; otherwise 5), then follow instructions on card. If the greenhouse level changes, migrate biomes. If more than one biome moves into a slot, the lower climax dies. If the hex capacity of four is exceeded, the lower climax reduces biomass. If a bridge clears with immigrants trapped behind it, place them on the map as described above.
B. Express Cards
DNA:
May add your DNA cards in hand to available genotype slots.
Genotypes:
Trigger a Malankovich Cycle, then derive new genotype from parents, replacing a single parent tent and copying all legal DNA from parent to young (respecting size restrictions and incompatibilities).
C. Recess DNA.
Decide to move DNA cards from your sheet to your hand. This removes DNA tents from genotype cards using them.
D. Adjust Trophic Level and Size.
Decide whether to be herbivore or predator. Then may change size by one step. Choose any two DNA if WW is expressed.
E. Population Expansion.
To your groups of similar tents in each biome or triangle, add a tent.
F. Herbivore Migrations.
Move your herbivore tents to a suitable biome.
G. Carnivore Migrations and Final Size.
May move your carnivore tents to a triangle with suitable prey. May adjust each carnivore size by one level.
H. Herbivore Cull.
In each over-capacity biome, keep applying culling rules (listed in order of importance) until the population equals biomass:
Niche:
Niche winners beat others.
Roadrunner:
Herbivore in hex inedible to all predators in the hex beat others.
Game Warden:
If there is only one type of predator and that predator can predate all herbivores in the hex, then the predator decides who survives.
Dentition:
Herbivores with the most teeth survive.
I. Carnivore Cull.
Predators without suitable prey die. If more predator tents then prey, larger predators beat smaller. If tied, fewer teeth beat more teeth.
Player Order
Any player may request that any phase (map setup, move, trophic) be done in the player order from the most r teeth to the least r teeth (i.e., mmm last).
Biome Card Placement
Latitude:
Biomes go into latitude row listed.
Orogeny:
Must go into mountain-building slot.
Marine:
Cannot go into mountain-building slot.
Stillborn:
If new climax is lower than that of all legal slots, the new biome is discarded as extinct.
Ties:
If there is more than one empty legal slot, place biome in the farthest south or west.
Immigrant Card Placement
W DNA:
Immigrants will change W to any DNA (except H) as required for suitability or niche.
Trophic:
Six-tooth immigrants = herbivores. Single-tooth = predators. Herbivores will check all biomes for suitability, checking triangles only if no suitable biomes exist. Predators will check triangles first, then biomes.
No competition:
Immigrants will bypass biomes or triangles containing other immigrants.
Biome Suitability
A herbivore cannot utilize a biome unless it has all the DNA requirements listed.
Prey Suitability
Size:
The predator cannot be larger than its prey, nor be more than two smaller.
Speed:
The prey must not be faster than the predator (i.e., have more S DNA).
Nocturnal:
The prey must not be more nocturnal than the predator (i.e., have more N DNA).
Armor:
The prey must not have more armor (a DNA) than the predator has armor-piercing (A DNA).
Animal Migration Limitations
Range:
Genotypes may migrate one hex, or two if size three or more.
Wings:
Migrate up to three hexes.
Sea:
Hexes with both slots marine or flooded can be entered only by using M DNA or wings. Animals with more than one M DNA are restricted to marine or flooded slots.
Ice:
Hexes with ice-sheets can be entered only by animals with PP DNA or wings.
Bridge:
If flooded, need MM DNA or wings.
Animal Extinction
Genotypes with no population discard the genotype and non-shared DNA cards. Shared DNA cards are moved to the columns of a genotype using them, even if this slot is full.
Victory
At the end of the Mesozoic (K-T), and after the last turn of the game, each player sums population. The player with the highest total wins.
